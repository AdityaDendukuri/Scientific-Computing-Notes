6  Linearity and Fundamental Subspaces

Linear systems recur throughout scientific computing. Methods for nonlinear equations, optimization, differential equations, and physical simulation routinely reduce a larger problem to a sequence of linear solves. Their reliable solution begins with the existence, uniqueness, and geometric structure encoded by the coefficient matrix.

6.1 Linear Systems and Geometric Interpretations

A matrix equation \(\bA\bx = \bb\) expresses the requirement that a data vector \(\bb\) be produced by a linear combination of the columns of \(\bA\), weighted by the unknown coordinates \(\bx\).

NoteDefinition: Linear System

A linear system of \(m\) equations in \(n\) unknowns is written in matrix-vector form as \[ \begin{align} \bA\bx = \bb, \end{align} \] where \(\bA \in \fR^{m \times n}\) is the coefficient matrix, \(\bx \in \fR^n\) is the unknown solution vector, and \(\bb \in \fR^m\) is the right-hand side vector.

TipRemark

(Row versus column perspectives) There are two complementary geometric interpretations of \(\bA\bx = \bb\):

  1. Row view: Each row \(\ba_i^T\bx = b_i\) defines an affine hyperplane in \(\fR^n\). Solving the system means locating the mutual intersection of these \(m\) hyperplanes.

  2. Column view: Partitioning \(\bA = [\ba_1, ..., \ba_n]\) into its columns, the product evaluates to \(\bA\bx = \sum_{j=1}^n x_j \ba_j\). Solving the system means expressing the vector \(\bb\) as a linear combination of the column vectors of \(\bA\).

NoteTheorem: Superposition Principle

Let \(\bA \in \fR^{m \times n}\) define a linear operator. If \(\bA\bx_1 = \bb_1\) and \(\bA\bx_2 = \bb_2\), then for any scalars \(\alpha, \beta \in \fR\), \[ \begin{align} \bA(\alpha\bx_1 + \beta\bx_2) = \alpha\bb_1 + \beta\bb_2. \end{align} \] Consequently, the solution to a linear system with a composite source term decomposes into a linear combination of individual response vectors.

TipProof

Using the distributivity and scalar associativity of matrix multiplication, \[ \begin{align} \bA(\alpha\bx_1 + \beta\bx_2) = \bA(\alpha\bx_1) + \bA(\beta\bx_2) = \alpha(\bA\bx_1) + \beta(\bA\bx_2) = \alpha\bb_1 + \beta\bb_2. \end{align} \]

6.2 Invertibility and the Four Fundamental Subspaces

The solvability and uniqueness of solutions are governed by four fundamental subspaces associated with \(\bA\).

NoteDefinition: The Four Fundamental Subspaces

Let \(\bA \in \fR^{m \times n}\).

  1. The Column Space (Range) \(\mathcal{R}(\bA) = \{ \bA\bx : \bx \in \fR^n \} \subseteq \fR^m\).

  2. The Null Space (Kernel) \(\mathcal{N}(\bA) = \{ \bx \in \fR^n : \bA\bx = \bzero \} \subseteq \fR^n\).

  3. The Row Space \(\mathcal{R}(\bA^T) = \{ \bA^T\by : \by \in \fR^m \} \subseteq \fR^n\).

  4. The Left Null Space \(\mathcal{N}(\bA^T) = \{ \by \in \fR^m : \bA^T\by = \bzero \} \subseteq \fR^m\).

The rank \(r = \operatorname{rank}(\bA)\) is the dimension of the column space \(\mathcal{R}(\bA)\).

NoteTheorem: The Rank-Nullity Theorem

Let \(\bA \in \fR^{m \times n}\) with rank \(r = \dim \mathcal{R}(\bA)\). Then \[ \begin{align} \operatorname{rank}(\bA) + \dim \mathcal{N}(\bA) = n. \end{align} \]

TipProof

Let \(k = \dim \mathcal{N}(\bA)\), and choose a basis \(\{\bv_1, ..., \bv_k\}\) for the null space. Extend this list to a basis \(\{\bv_1, ..., \bv_n\}\) of \(\fR^n\). For any \(\bx \in \fR^n\), expand \(\bx = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \bv_i\). Linearity gives \[ \begin{align} \bA\bx = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i \bA\bv_i + \sum_{i=k+1}^n c_i \bA\bv_i = \bzero + \sum_{i=k+1}^n c_i \bA\bv_i = \sum_{i=k+1}^n c_i \bA\bv_i. \end{align} \] Thus the vectors \(\{\bA\bv_{k+1}, ..., \bA\bv_n\}\) span \(\mathcal{R}(\bA)\). To establish linear independence, suppose \(\sum_{i=k+1}^n d_i \bA\bv_i = \bzero\). Linearity yields \[ \begin{align} \bA\left(\sum_{i=k+1}^n d_i \bv_i\right) = \bzero \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \bw = \sum_{i=k+1}^n d_i \bv_i \in \mathcal{N}(\bA). \end{align} \] Because \(\{\bv_1, ..., \bv_k\}\) is a basis for \(\mathcal{N}(\bA)\), there exist coefficients \(a_1, ..., a_k\) such that \(\bw = \sum_{j=1}^k a_j \bv_j\). Equating the two expressions gives \[ \begin{align} \sum_{j=1}^k a_j \bv_j - \sum_{i=k+1}^n d_i \bv_i = \bzero. \end{align} \] Since the full set \(\{\bv_1, ..., \bv_n\}\) is linearly independent, every coefficient vanishes: \[ \begin{align} a_1 = ... = a_k = d_{k+1} = ... = d_n = 0. \end{align} \] Therefore \(\{\bA\bv_{k+1}, ..., \bA\bv_n\}\) is a basis for \(\mathcal{R}(\bA)\), containing exactly \(n - k\) vectors. Hence \[ \begin{align} r = n - k = n - \dim\mathcal{N}(\bA) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \operatorname{rank}(\bA) + \dim \mathcal{N}(\bA) = n. \end{align} \]

NoteTheorem: Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra and Orthogonal Decompositions

For any matrix \(\bA \in \fR^{m \times n}\):

  1. The row space and null space are orthogonal complements in \(\fR^n\): \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{R}(\bA^T) \perp \mathcal{N}(\bA) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \fR^n = \mathcal{R}(\bA^T) \oplus \mathcal{N}(\bA). \end{align} \]

  2. The column space and left null space are orthogonal complements in \(\fR^m\): \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{R}(\bA) \perp \mathcal{N}(\bA^T) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \fR^m = \mathcal{R}(\bA) \oplus \mathcal{N}(\bA^T). \end{align} \]

Consequently, \(\dim \mathcal{R}(\bA^T) = \dim \mathcal{R}(\bA) = \operatorname{rank}(\bA)\).

TipProof

For any \(\bx\in\fR^n\), the condition \(\bA\bx=\bzero\) says that every row of \(\bA\) has zero inner product with \(\bx\). Since those rows span \(\mathcal{R}(\bA^T)\), \[ \begin{align} \bx\in\mathcal{N}(\bA) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bx\in\mathcal{R}(\bA^T)^\perp. \end{align} \] Taking orthogonal complements in finite-dimensional space gives \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{R}(\bA^T)=\mathcal{N}(\bA)^\perp. \end{align} \] The identical argument with \(\bA^T\) gives \(\mathcal{R}(\bA)=\mathcal{N}(\bA^T)^\perp\). Each space therefore decomposes into one of these subspaces and its orthogonal complement. Finally, rank–nullity gives \[ \begin{align} \dim\mathcal{R}(\bA^T) =n-\dim\mathcal{N}(\bA) =\operatorname{rank}(\bA), \end{align} \] so row rank equals column rank.

NoteTheorem: Solvability and the Left Null Space

The linear system \(\bA\bx = \bb\) has a solution if and only if \(\bb \in \mathcal{R}(\bA)\). Equivalently, \(\bA\bx = \bb\) is solvable if and only if \(\bb\) is orthogonal to every vector in the left null space of \(\bA\): \[ \begin{align} \bA\bx = \bb \text{ has a solution} \iff \by^T\bb = 0 \quad \text{for all } \by \text{ such that } \bA^T\by = \bzero. \end{align} \] If a solution exists and \(\bx_p\) is one particular solution, the complete set of solutions is the affine space \[ \begin{align} \bx = \bx_p + \bz, \qquad \bz \in \mathcal{N}(\bA). \end{align} \]

TipProof

By the referenced result, \(\fR^m = \mathcal{R}(\bA) \oplus \mathcal{N}(\bA^T)\). Therefore \(\bb \in \mathcal{R}(\bA)\) if and only if \(\bb \perp \mathcal{N}(\bA^T)\), which means \[ \begin{align} \langle \by, \bb \rangle = \by^T\bb = 0 \qquad \text{for all } \by \in \mathcal{N}(\bA^T). \end{align} \] If \(\bA\bx_p = \bb\), then for any \(\bz \in \mathcal{N}(\bA)\), \[ \begin{align} \bA(\bx_p + \bz) = \bA\bx_p + \bA\bz = \bb + \bzero = \bb. \end{align} \] Conversely, if \(\bA\bx = \bb\), then \[ \begin{align} \bA(\bx - \bx_p) = \bA\bx - \bA\bx_p = \bb - \bb = \bzero, \end{align} \] which implies \(\bx - \bx_p \in \mathcal{N}(\bA)\), so \(\bx = \bx_p + \bz\) with \(\bz \in \mathcal{N}(\bA)\).

TipRemark

This compatibility condition is often called the finite-dimensional Fredholm alternative. Each left-null vector describes one obstruction that a solvable right-hand side must avoid.

6.3 Exercises

WarningExercise

  1. Compute. Dimensions and bases for the four fundamental subspaces. Let \[ \begin{align} \bA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 & 2 & 7 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \]

    1. Compute the row reduced echelon form of \(\bA\). Determine the rank \(r = \operatorname{rank}(\bA)\).

    2. Find bases for \(\mathcal{R}(\bA)\), \(\mathcal{N}(\bA)\), \(\mathcal{R}(\bA^T)\), and \(\mathcal{N}(\bA^T)\). After studying QR factorization, return and replace them by orthonormal bases.

    3. Confirm that \(\dim \mathcal{R}(\bA) + \dim \mathcal{N}(\bA^T) = 3\) and \(\dim \mathcal{R}(\bA^T) + \dim \mathcal{N}(\bA) = 4\).

    4. Verify numerically that every vector in your basis of \(\mathcal{R}(\bA^T)\) is orthogonal to every vector in your basis of \(\mathcal{N}(\bA)\).

  2. Derive. Decomposition into row space and null space components. Let \(\bA \in \fR^{m \times n}\).

    1. Prove that every vector \(\bx \in \fR^n\) has a unique orthogonal decomposition \(\bx = \bx_{\text{row}} + \bx_{\text{null}}\) with \(\bx_{\text{row}} \in \mathcal{R}(\bA^T)\) and \(\bx_{\text{null}} \in \mathcal{N}(\bA)\).

    2. Show that \(\bA\bx = \bA\bx_{\text{row}}\), demonstrating that the null-space component contributes nothing to the output.

    3. Prove that among all solutions to a consistent underdetermined system \(\bA\bx = \bb\), the unique solution of minimal Euclidean norm \(\|\bx\|_2\) lies strictly in the row space: \(\bx_{\text{min}} \in \mathcal{R}(\bA^T)\).

  3. Derive. The Fredholm alternative and inconsistent systems. Consider the \(3 \times 2\) system \[ \begin{align} \bA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \bb = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ b_3 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \]

    1. Compute a basis for the left null space \(\mathcal{N}(\bA^T)\).

    2. Apply the referenced result to find the exact value of \(b_3\) that makes the system consistent.

    3. For \(b_3 = 5\), find the orthogonal projection of \(\bb\) onto \(\mathcal{R}(\bA)\) and compute the minimum residual norm \(\min_{\bx} \|\bA\bx - \bb\|_2\).

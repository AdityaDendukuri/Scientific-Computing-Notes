9  Symmetric Positive Definite Matrices

Symmetric positive definite matrices occur whenever a nonzero displacement must have positive energy. Springs, diffusion operators, covariance models, and strictly convex quadratic objectives all lead to this structure. For these matrices an algebraic condition, a geometry, and an efficient algorithm describe the same property.

9.1 Positive Quadratic Energy

NoteDefinition: Symmetric Positive Definite Matrix

A real matrix \(\bA\in\fR^{n\times n}\) is symmetric positive definite, abbreviated SPD, if \(\bA=\bA^T\) and \[ \begin{align} \bx^T\bA\bx>0 \qquad\text{for every }\bx\neq\bzero. \end{align} \] It is symmetric positive semidefinite if the strict inequality is replaced by \(\bx^T\bA\bx\geq0\).

NoteTheorem: First Consequences of Positive Definiteness

If \(\bA\) is SPD, then every diagonal entry is positive, every principal submatrix is SPD, and \(\bA\) is nonsingular.

TipProof

Taking \(\bx=\be_i\) gives \(a_{ii}=\be_i^T\bA\be_i>0\). A vector supported only on an index set \(I\) has quadratic energy equal to the quadratic form of the principal submatrix \(\bA[I,I]\), so that submatrix is positive definite. Finally, if \(\bA\bx=\bzero\) for a nonzero \(\bx\), then \(\bx^T\bA\bx=0\), a contradiction. Thus the null space is trivial.

NoteDefinition: The \(\bA\)-Inner Product and Energy Norm

If \(\bA\) is SPD, then \[ \begin{align} \langle\bx,\by\rangle_{\bA}=\bx^T\bA\by, \qquad \|\bx\|_{\bA}=\sqrt{\bx^T\bA\bx} \end{align} \] define an inner product and its induced norm. The scalar \(\bx^T\bA\bx\) is often called the energy of \(\bx\).

TipProof

Linearity follows from matrix multiplication, symmetry from \(\bA^T=\bA\), and positive definiteness from the definition of SPD. The general inner-product results of the referenced section then give Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality for \(\|\cdot\|_{\bA}\).

NoteExample

For \[ \begin{align} \bA=\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, \end{align} \] the unit-energy curve is \(4x_1^2+x_2^2=1\). A displacement in the first coordinate costs four times as much energy as the same displacement in the second. A norm chosen this way also expresses which errors matter in the model.

9.2 Cholesky Factorization

NoteTheorem: Cholesky Factorization

Every SPD matrix \(\bA\in\fR^{n\times n}\) has a unique factorization \[ \begin{align} \bA=\bL\bL^T, \end{align} \] where \(\bL\) is lower triangular with positive diagonal entries.

TipProof

We proceed by induction on \(n\). For \(n=1\), \(\bA = (\alpha)\) with \(\alpha > 0\), so \(\bL = (\sqrt{\alpha})\) is the unique positive square root. For the induction step, partition \(\bA \in \fR^{n \times n}\) as \[ \begin{align} \bA = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \br^T \\ \br & \bB \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \] Since \(\bA\) is SPD, \(\alpha = \be_1^T\bA\be_1 > 0\). Set \(\ell_{11} = \sqrt{\alpha}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\ell} = \br / \ell_{11}\). Form the Schur complement \[ \begin{align} \bS = \bB - \boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{\ell}^T = \bB - \frac{\br\br^T}{\alpha}. \end{align} \] For any nonzero \(\by \in \fR^{n-1}\), choose \(\bx = (-\boldsymbol{\ell}^T\by / \ell_{11}, \by^T)^T \in \fR^n\). Expanding the quadratic form: \[ \begin{align} \bx^T\bA\bx &= \begin{pmatrix} -\frac{\boldsymbol{\ell}^T\by}{\ell_{11}} \\ \by \end{pmatrix}^T \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \br^T \\ \br & \bB \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -\frac{\boldsymbol{\ell}^T\by}{\ell_{11}} \\ \by \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \alpha\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\ell}^T\by}{\ell_{11}}\right)^2 - 2\frac{(\boldsymbol{\ell}^T\by)(\br^T\by)}{\ell_{11}} + \by^T\bB\by \\ &= (\boldsymbol{\ell}^T\by)^2 - 2(\boldsymbol{\ell}^T\by)^2 + \by^T\bB\by \\ &= \by^T(\bB - \boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{\ell}^T)\by = \by^T\bS\by. \end{align} \] Since \(\by \neq \bzero\), the vector \(\bx\) is nonzero, so \(\by^T\bS\by = \bx^T\bA\bx > 0\). Hence \(\bS\) is SPD. By induction, \(\bS = \bK\bK^T\) for a unique lower triangular \(\bK\) with positive diagonal entries. Block multiplication gives \[ \begin{align} \bA = \begin{pmatrix} \ell_{11} & \bzero^T \\ \boldsymbol{\ell} & \bK \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \ell_{11} & \boldsymbol{\ell}^T \\ \bzero & \bK^T \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \ell_{11}^2 & \ell_{11}\boldsymbol{\ell}^T \\ \ell_{11}\boldsymbol{\ell} & \boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{\ell}^T + \bK\bK^T \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \br^T \\ \br & \bB \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \] The positivity of the diagonal requires \(\ell_{11} = \sqrt{\alpha}\), which uniquely fixes \(\boldsymbol{\ell} = \br / \ell_{11}\), and uniqueness of \(\bK\) makes the entire factor \(\bL\) unique.

NoteTheorem: Equivalent Characterizations of Positive Definiteness

For a real symmetric matrix \(\bA\), the following statements are equivalent.

  1. \(\bx^T\bA\bx>0\) for every nonzero \(\bx\).

  2. \(\bA=\bL\bL^T\) for a lower triangular \(\bL\) with positive diagonal.

  3. \(\bA=\bW^T\bW\) for a nonsingular matrix \(\bW\).

  4. Every leading principal minor is positive.

TipProof

The implication from (1) to (2) is the referenced result. Statement (2) implies (3) by taking \(\bW=\bL^T\). If (3) holds, then \[ \begin{align} \bx^T\bA\bx=\|\bW\bx\|_2^2>0 \end{align} \] for every nonzero \(\bx\), so (3) implies (1).

If (2) holds, the leading \(k\) by \(k\) block satisfies \(\bA_k=\bL_k\bL_k^T\). Hence \[ \begin{align} \det(\bA_k)=\det(\bL_k)^2 =\prod_{j=1}^k\ell_{jj}^2>0, \end{align} \] which proves (4). Conversely, suppose every leading principal minor is positive. Apply the Cholesky formulas in order. At step \(k\), the next squared pivot \(v_k\) satisfies \[ \begin{align} \det(\bA_k)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\ell_{jj}^2\right)v_k. \end{align} \] The determinant on the left and the preceding factors are positive, so \(v_k>0\). Thus every square root exists and the algorithm produces (2).

TipRemark

After the spectral theorem is available, one more characterization follows directly. A real symmetric matrix is SPD exactly when all its eigenvalues are positive. The Cholesky argument does not need this fact, so we postpone it and keep the present argument self-contained.

9.3 The Cholesky Algorithm

NoteTheorem: The Cholesky Algorithm

The entries of the factor in \(\bA=\bL\bL^T\) are computed column by column: \[ \begin{align} \ell_{jj} &=\sqrt{a_{jj}-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\ell_{jk}^2},\\ \ell_{ij} &=\frac{a_{ij}-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\ell_{ik}\ell_{jk}}{\ell_{jj}}, \qquad i=j+1,...,n. \end{align} \] For an SPD matrix every expression under a square root is positive. A nonpositive pivot signals that the input is not positive definite, apart from the effects of rounding.

TipProof

For \(i\geq j\), equating entry \((i,j)\) in \(\bA\) and \(\bL\bL^T\) gives \[ \begin{align} a_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\ell_{ik}\ell_{jk}. \end{align} \] When \(i=j\), solve this identity for the positive diagonal entry. When \(i>j\), solve it for \(\ell_{ij}\). The order of computation ensures that all earlier terms are already known.

NoteExample

For \[ \begin{align} \bA=\begin{pmatrix}4&2&-2\\2&10&2\\-2&2&6\end{pmatrix}, \end{align} \] the algorithm gives \[ \begin{align} \ell_{11}=2,&\quad \ell_{21}=1,\quad \ell_{31}=-1,\\ \ell_{22}=3,&\quad \ell_{32}=1,\quad \ell_{33}=2. \end{align} \] Thus \[ \begin{align} \bL=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\1&3&0\\-1&1&2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \bL\bL^T=\bA. \end{align} \] To solve \(\bA\bx=\bb\), first solve \(\bL\by=\bb\) and then \(\bL^T\bx=\by\).

9.4 Cost and Stability

NoteTheorem: Flop Count and Storage

Dense Cholesky factorization requires \(n^3/3+O(n^2)\) floating-point operations. Only one triangular half of the matrix and factor need be stored. The two triangular solves for one right-hand side require \(2n^2+O(n)\) further operations.

TipProof

Column \(j\) updates \(n-j+1\) entries using inner products of length about \(j\). Summing these costs gives \[ \begin{align} \sum_{j=1}^n 2(n-j)j=\frac{n^3}{3}+O(n^2). \end{align} \] The leading cost is half that of dense LU factorization.

TipRemark

Cholesky needs no pivoting for an SPD matrix. Moreover, the identity \[ \begin{align} a_{ii}=\sum_{k=1}^i\ell_{ik}^2 \end{align} \] implies \(|\ell_{ik}|\leq\sqrt{a_{ii}}\), so the factor cannot exhibit the element growth possible in general elimination. In standard floating-point arithmetic, and absent overflow or underflow, the computed factor is the exact Cholesky factor of a nearby symmetric matrix. This is the appropriate backward-stability statement. It does not say that an ill-conditioned system has an accurate forward solution.

TipRemark

Once singular values are available, the referenced result gives \[ \begin{align} \kappa_2(\bA)=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\bA)}{\lambda_{\min}(\bA)}, \qquad \kappa_2(\bL)=\sqrt{\kappa_2(\bA)}. \end{align} \] These are spectral interpretations of structure already visible in the energy and the factorization.

9.5 Sparse Cholesky and the Elimination Graph

For a sparse SPD matrix, the Cholesky factor can have many more nonzero entries than the matrix itself. These new entries are called fill. Fill determines the memory and the arithmetic of a sparse factorization, and it depends strongly on the order in which the unknowns are eliminated. A graph associated with the matrix predicts the fill before any arithmetic is done.

NoteDefinition: Graph of a Symmetric Matrix

The graph of a symmetric matrix \(\bA\in\fR^{n\times n}\) has vertices \(1,...,n\) and an edge \(\{i,j\}\) for each pair \(i\neq j\) with \(a_{ij}\neq0\). The neighbors of a vertex \(v\) are the vertices joined to \(v\) by an edge, and the degree of \(v\) is the number of its neighbors.

NoteTheorem: Elimination as a Graph Operation

Let \(\bA\) be SPD with graph \(G\), and partition \(\bA\) as in the proof of the referenced result, with first column \((\alpha,\br^T)^T\) and trailing block \(\bB\). The first column of \(\bL\) is nonzero exactly in the rows of the neighbors of vertex \(1\). Apart from exact numerical cancellation, the graph of the Schur complement \(\bS=\bB-\br\br^T/\alpha\) is obtained from \(G\) by deleting vertex \(1\) and joining every pair of its neighbors by an edge.

TipProof

The first column of \(\bL\) is \((\sqrt{\alpha},\br^T/\sqrt{\alpha})^T\), and \(r_i\neq0\) exactly when \(i\) is a neighbor of vertex \(1\). The entries of the Schur complement are \(s_{ij}=b_{ij}-r_ir_j/\alpha\). The correction \(r_ir_j/\alpha\) is nonzero exactly when both \(i\) and \(j\) are neighbors of vertex \(1\). For such a pair, \(s_{ij}\) is nonzero unless \(b_{ij}\) happens to equal \(r_ir_j/\alpha\), and for every other pair \(s_{ij}=b_{ij}\).

Applying the referenced result repeatedly describes the whole factorization. Eliminating a vertex turns its remaining neighbors into a clique, and the nonzero pattern of column \(k\) of \(\bL\) is the set of neighbors of vertex \(k\) at the moment it is eliminated. Reordering the unknowns by a permutation \(\bP\) and factoring \(\bP\bA\bP^T\) changes the elimination order and therefore the fill, without changing the solution.

NoteExample

(An arrow matrix) Let \(\bA\) be SPD with nonzero entries only on the diagonal and in the first row and column. Its graph is a star with center \(1\). If the center is eliminated first, its \(n-1\) neighbors become a clique, the Schur complement is full, and \(\bL\) has about \(n^2/2\) nonzero entries. If the center is eliminated last, every leaf has the center as its only neighbor, no edges are added, and \(\bL\) has \(2n-1\) nonzero entries. The two orders give the same solution at costs of \(O(n^3)\) and \(O(n)\) flops.

TipRemark

(Fill-reducing orderings) Finding the ordering with the least fill is NP-complete (Yannakakis 1981), so practical codes use heuristics. The minimum degree heuristic always eliminates a vertex of smallest current degree, which bounds the fill created at each step by the square of that degree. The degrees change after every elimination, and an efficient implementation stores the vertices in a bucket queue, with one doubly linked list of vertices for each degree. Moving a vertex to a new bucket then takes constant time, and the next vertex is found by scanning upward from the lowest nonempty bucket (Davis 2006). Nested dissection instead removes a small set of vertices that separates the graph into two parts, orders the separator last, and recurses on the parts.

Nested dissection determines the cost of sparse Cholesky for the Poisson problem. On an \(N\times N\) grid with \(n=N^2\) unknowns, the natural row-by-row order gives a factor with bandwidth \(N\), so \(\bL\) has about \(n^{3/2}\) nonzero entries and the factorization costs about \(n^2\) flops. Nested dissection with grid lines as separators reduces these to \(O(n\log n)\) nonzero entries and \(O(n^{3/2})\) flops (George 1973). On an \(N\times N\times N\) grid, separators are planes with \(n^{2/3}\) vertices, and nested dissection leaves \(O(n^{4/3})\) nonzero entries and \(O(n^2)\) flops. The growth of the factor with \(n\) is the reason that large three-dimensional problems are solved by the iterative methods of the referenced section.

9.6 Exercises

WarningExercise

  1. Compute. Compute the Cholesky factor of \[ \begin{align} \bA=\begin{pmatrix}9&3&-6\\3&26&-7\\-6&-7&9\end{pmatrix} \end{align} \] by hand, then use it to solve \(\bA\bx=(6,44,2)^T\).

  2. Derive. Prove directly from the quadratic form that every principal submatrix of an SPD matrix is SPD. Explain why the same statement for an arbitrary submatrix is meaningless.

  3. Derive. Complete the block calculation in the proof of the referenced result: verify exactly that \(\bx^T\bA\bx=\by^T\bS\by\). Then prove the converse statement that if \(\alpha>0\) and \(\bS\) is SPD, the block matrix \(\bA\) is SPD.

  4. Derive. Prove that the largest entry of an SPD matrix in absolute value lies on the main diagonal. Begin with a two by two principal submatrix and choose a useful vector, or use its positive determinant.

  5. Derive. Let \(\bX\in\fR^{m\times n}\) with \(m\geq n\). Prove that \(\bX^T\bX\) is SPD exactly when the columns of \(\bX\) are linearly independent. State what remains true when they are dependent.

  6. Check. For the arrow matrix of the example with \(n=5\), draw the elimination graphs for the two orders, and count the nonzero entries of \(\bL\) in each case.

  7. Derive. Use the referenced result to prove that, apart from cancellation, \(\ell_{ij}\neq0\) for \(i>j\) exactly when \(G\) contains a path from \(i\) to \(j\) whose interior vertices all precede \(j\) in the elimination order.

  8. Compute. Assemble the five-point Poisson matrix on an \(N\times N\) grid for \(N=20,40,80\). Factor it with a sparse direct solver using the natural order and a minimum degree order, and report the number of nonzero entries in the factors and the factorization times.

  9. Explore. Show that \(\|\bx\|_{\bA}=\|\bL^T\bx\|_2\). Use this identity to interpret the \(\bA\)-unit sphere as the inverse image of the Euclidean unit sphere, without mentioning eigenvalues.

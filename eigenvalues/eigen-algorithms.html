14  Vector Iterations and the QR Algorithm

the referenced section characterized eigenvalues but did not compute them. Every polynomial is the characteristic polynomial of its companion matrix, so the Abel–Ruffini theorem implies that no formula built from finitely many arithmetic operations and root extractions gives the eigenvalues of a general matrix with \(n\geq5\). Every eigenvalue algorithm is therefore iterative. We first study iterations that apply \(\bA\) or \((\bA-\sigma\bI)^{-1}\) to a single vector. These iterations compute one eigenpair at a time and form the basis of the sparse methods in later sections. We then describe the QR algorithm, which computes all eigenvalues of a dense matrix by a sequence of orthogonal similarity transformations.

14.1 Power Iteration and Shifted Inverse Iteration

NoteDefinition: Power Iteration

Let \(\bA\) be real symmetric. Given a unit vector \(\bv^{(0)}\), power iteration constructs \[ \begin{align} \bw^{(k)} = \bA \bv^{(k-1)}, \qquad \bv^{(k)} = \frac{\bw^{(k)}}{\|\bw^{(k)}\|_2}, \qquad \lambda^{(k)} = (\bv^{(k)})^T \bA \bv^{(k)}. \end{align} \] Order the eigenvalues by magnitude. If \(|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\geq\cdots\geq|\lambda_n|\) and \(\bq_1^T\bv^{(0)}\neq0\), the direction of the iterate converges linearly: \[ \begin{align} \|\bv^{(k)} - (\pm \bq_1)\|_2 = O\left( \left| \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} \right|^k \right). \end{align} \] For a general nonnormal matrix, an eigenvector basis may be poorly conditioned or may not exist, and this simple orthogonal expansion no longer justifies the claim.

NoteDefinition: Shifted Inverse Iteration

For a real symmetric matrix, an eigenvalue near a target \(\sigma\) can be isolated by applying power iteration implicitly to \((\bA-\sigma\bI)^{-1}\): \[ \begin{align} (\bA - \sigma \bI) \bw^{(k)} = \bv^{(k-1)}, \qquad \bv^{(k)} = \frac{\bw^{(k)}}{\|\bw^{(k)}\|_2}. \end{align} \] The eigenvalues of \((\bA - \sigma \bI)^{-1}\) are \((\lambda_i - \sigma)^{-1}\). Provided the shift is not itself an eigenvalue and the starting vector contains the desired component, the mode closest to \(\sigma\) dominates. The matrix inverse is never formed. Each step solves a shifted linear system instead.

NoteDefinition: Rayleigh Quotient Iteration (RQI)

For symmetric matrices, updating the shift dynamically to the current Rayleigh estimate \(\sigma_k = (\bv^{(k)})^T \bA \bv^{(k)}\) defines Rayleigh Quotient Iteration: \[ \begin{align} (\bA - \sigma_k \bI) \bw^{(k+1)} = \bv^{(k)}, \qquad \bv^{(k+1)} = \frac{\bw^{(k+1)}}{\|\bw^{(k+1)}\|_2}, \qquad \sigma_{k+1} = (\bv^{(k+1)})^T \bA \bv^{(k+1)}. \end{align} \]

NoteTheorem: Local Cubic Convergence of Rayleigh Quotient Iteration

Let \(\bA\) be real symmetric, and let \(\lambda_*\) be a simple eigenvalue with unit eigenvector \(\bq_*\). If a unit RQI iterate \(\bv^{(k)}\) is sufficiently close to \(\bq_*\), then \[ \begin{align} \sin\angle(\bv^{(k+1)},\bq_*) \leq C\sin^3\angle(\bv^{(k)},\bq_*). \end{align} \] If \(\lambda_*\) is an extremal eigenvalue, the Rayleigh shifts also satisfy \[ \begin{align} |\sigma_{k+1}-\lambda_*| \leq C'|\sigma_k-\lambda_*|^3 \end{align} \] for iterates sufficiently close to \(\bq_*\).

TipProof

Write the current unit vector as \[ \begin{align} \bv=\cos\theta\,\bq_*+\sin\theta\,\bz, \qquad \bz\perp\bq_*, \qquad \|\bz\|_2=1. \end{align} \] Its Rayleigh quotient \(\sigma=\bv^T\bA\bv\) obeys \[ \begin{align} \sigma &= (\cos\theta\,\bq_*+\sin\theta\,\bz)^T\bA(\cos\theta\,\bq_*+\sin\theta\,\bz) \\ &= \cos^2\theta\,\lambda_* + 2\cos\theta\sin\theta\,\bq_*^T\bA\bz + \sin^2\theta\,\bz^T\bA\bz. \end{align} \] Because \(\bA\bq_* = \lambda_*\bq_*\) and \(\bq_*^T\bz = 0\), the cross-term vanishes: \(\bq_*^T\bA\bz = \lambda_*\bq_*^T\bz = 0\). Substituting \(\cos^2\theta = 1-\sin^2\theta\), \[ \begin{align} \sigma-\lambda_* = (1-\sin^2\theta)\lambda_* + \sin^2\theta\,\bz^T\bA\bz - \lambda_* = \sin^2\theta\,(\bz^T\bA\bz-\lambda_*), \end{align} \] so \(|\sigma-\lambda_*|\leq C_0\sin^2\theta\).

Let \(\gamma=\min_{\lambda_j\neq\lambda_*}|\lambda_j-\lambda_*|>0\). Once \(\theta\) is small enough, \(|\lambda_j-\sigma|\geq\gamma/2\) for every other eigenvalue. Expanding the inverse-iteration vector \(\bw=(\bA-\sigma\bI)^{-1}\bv\) in an orthonormal eigenbasis, its component along \(\bq_*\) has magnitude \[ \begin{align} \frac{|\cos\theta|}{|\lambda_*-\sigma|}, \end{align} \] whereas the norm of its orthogonal component is at most \(2|\sin\theta|/\gamma\). Normalization does not change their ratio. Therefore, \[ \begin{align} \tan\angle(\bv^+,\bq_*) \leq \frac{2|\lambda_*-\sigma|}{\gamma|\cos\theta|}|\sin\theta| \leq C_1|\sin\theta|^3. \end{align} \] For small angles, \(\sin\theta\) and \(\tan\theta\) are comparable, which proves cubic convergence of the eigenvector direction.

If \(\lambda_*\) is extremal, the spectral gap also gives two-sided local bounds \(c\sin^2\theta\leq|\sigma-\lambda_*|\leq C\sin^2\theta\). Applying the Rayleigh-quotient identity to the next iterate and using the cubic angle bound yields \[ \begin{align} |\sigma^+-\lambda_*| \leq C_2\sin^2\angle(\bv^+,\bq_*) \leq C_3\sin^6\theta \leq C_4|\sigma-\lambda_*|^3. \end{align} \]

14.2 The Practical QR Algorithm

NoteDefinition: Basic QR Iteration

Given \(\bA_0 = \bA\), the unshifted QR iteration generates orthogonally similar matrices via \[ \begin{align} \bA_k = \bQ_k \bR_k, \qquad \bA_{k+1} = \bR_k \bQ_k = \bQ_k^T \bA_k \bQ_k. \end{align} \] All iterates share the same eigenvalues. Under suitable separation conditions the subdiagonal entries tend to zero and the iterates reveal a Schur form. Shifts and deflation are essential for a reliable practical method.

NoteLemma: Invariance of Hessenberg Structure under QR Iteration

Let \(\bH \in \fR^{n \times n}\) be an upper Hessenberg matrix (\(H_{ij} = 0\) for all \(i > j + 1\)). If \(\bH = \bQ\bR\) is the QR factorization of \(\bH\), then the reversed product \(\bH_+ = \bR\bQ\) is also upper Hessenberg.

TipProof

Because \(\bH = \bQ\bR\) and \(\bR\) is upper triangular and invertible (assuming \(\bH\) is nonsingular, or extending by continuity), \(\bQ = \bH \bR^{-1}\). Because the inverse of an upper triangular matrix is upper triangular, \(\bR^{-1}\) is upper triangular. The product of an upper Hessenberg matrix \(\bH\) and an upper triangular matrix \(\bR^{-1}\) is upper Hessenberg. Therefore, \(\bQ\) is upper Hessenberg.

Now consider \(\bH_+ = \bR\bQ\). The matrix \(\bR\) is upper triangular and \(\bQ\) is upper Hessenberg. For entry \((i, j)\) with \(i > j + 1\): \[ \begin{align} (\bH_+)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n R_{ik} Q_{kj}. \end{align} \] Because \(\bR\) is upper triangular, \(R_{ik} = 0\) for all \(k < i\). For the remaining terms \(k \geq i\), we have \(k \geq i > j + 1\), which implies \(k > j + 1\). Because \(\bQ\) is upper Hessenberg, \(Q_{kj} = 0\) for all \(k > j + 1\). Thus every term in the sum vanishes, proving \((\bH_+)_{ij} = 0\) for \(i > j + 1\).

NoteTheorem: Practical Shifted QR Framework

A standard dense nonsymmetric eigenvalue computation has two phases:

  1. Hessenberg reduction: Compute \(\bH_0=\bU_0^T\bA\bU_0\) with Householder reflectors. This one-time reduction costs \(O(n^3)\) operations and preserves the eigenvalues.

  2. Implicit shifted QR steps: Apply shifts while preserving Hessenberg form. A schematic single-shift step is \[ \begin{align} \bH_k - \mu_k \bI = \bQ_k \bR_k, \qquad \bH_{k+1} = \bR_k \bQ_k + \mu_k \bI. \end{align} \] the referenced result explains why each step costs \(O(n^2)\) rather than \(O(n^3)\). Real nonsymmetric codes use implicit single or double shifts so that complex conjugate pairs can remain in real arithmetic. A small subdiagonal entry permits deflation and splits the problem into smaller blocks.

For a real symmetric matrix, Hessenberg form reduces to tridiagonal form. Wilkinson shifts from the trailing two by two block and implicit bulge chasing then give a particularly efficient symmetric algorithm.

14.3 The Jacobi Eigenvalue Algorithm

For a symmetric matrix, an alternative to the QR algorithm drives the off-diagonal entries to zero directly. Each step applies an orthogonal similarity by a plane rotation chosen to annihilate one off-diagonal entry. The method, due to Jacobi in 1846, is slower than shifted QR for a general dense matrix. It remains useful because it can compute the small eigenvalues of graded positive definite matrices to high relative accuracy (Demmel 1997), and because rotations acting on disjoint pairs of indices can be applied in parallel.

NoteDefinition: Givens Plane Rotation Matrix

The matrix \(\bG(p, q, \theta) \in \fR^{n \times n}\) is an identity matrix except for the four entries at the intersections of rows and columns \(p\) and \(q\): \[ \begin{align} g_{pp} = c, \qquad g_{pq} = s, \qquad g_{qp} = -s, \qquad g_{qq} = c, \end{align} \] where \(c = \cos\theta\) and \(s = \sin\theta\). The transformation \(\bA' = \bG^T \bA \bG\) modifies only rows and columns \(p\) and \(q\) of \(\bA\).

NoteTheorem: Derivation of the Optimal Rotation Angle

To zero the transformed off-diagonal entry \(a'_{pq} = 0\), we consider the \(2 \times 2\) orthogonal similarity transformation on the submatrix at indices \(\{p, q\}\): \[ \begin{align} \begin{pmatrix} a'_{pp} & a'_{pq} \\ a'_{pq} & a'_{qq} \end{pmatrix} &= \begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a_{pp} & a_{pq} \\ a_{pq} & a_{qq} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c & s \\ -s & c \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} c^2 a_{pp} - 2cs a_{pq} + s^2 a_{qq} & (c^2 - s^2) a_{pq} + cs (a_{pp} - a_{qq}) \\ (c^2 - s^2) a_{pq} + cs (a_{pp} - a_{qq}) & s^2 a_{pp} + 2cs a_{pq} + c^2 a_{qq} \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \] Setting \(a'_{pq} = 0\) yields the trigonometric condition \[ \begin{align} (c^2 - s^2) a_{pq} + cs (a_{pp} - a_{qq}) = 0 \implies \frac{\cos(2\theta)}{\sin(2\theta)} = \frac{a_{qq} - a_{pp}}{2a_{pq}}. \end{align} \] Defining \(\tau = \cot(2\theta) = \frac{a_{qq} - a_{pp}}{2a_{pq}}\) and using \(\cot(2\theta) = \frac{1 - \tan^2\theta}{2\tan\theta}\), setting \(t = \tan\theta\) produces \[ \begin{align} t^2 + 2\tau t - 1 = 0. \end{align} \] To minimize the perturbation to \(\bA\) and avoid cancellation, we select the root of smaller absolute value: \[ \begin{align} t = \frac{\operatorname{sign}(\tau)}{|\tau| + \sqrt{1 + \tau^2}}, \qquad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + t^2}}, \qquad s = ct. \end{align} \]

TipProof

The quadratic formula yields roots \(t = -\tau \pm \sqrt{1 + \tau^2}\). Multiplying the root of smaller magnitude by its algebraic conjugate yields \[ \begin{align} t = \frac{(-\tau \pm \sqrt{1 + \tau^2})(\tau \pm \sqrt{1 + \tau^2})}{\tau \pm \sqrt{1 + \tau^2}} = \frac{1}{\tau + \operatorname{sign}(\tau)\sqrt{1 + \tau^2}} = \frac{\operatorname{sign}(\tau)}{|\tau| + \sqrt{1 + \tau^2}}. \end{align} \] This formula satisfies \(|t| \leq 1\), corresponding to a rotation angle \(|\theta| \leq \pi/4\), and avoids subtracting nearly equal floating-point numbers.

A rotation that annihilates \(a_{pq}\) may make a previously annihilated entry nonzero again, so the method does not terminate after \(n(n-1)/2\) steps. Convergence follows instead from a quantity that decreases at every step.

NoteTheorem: Monotone Decrease of the Off-Diagonal Frobenius Norm

Let the off-diagonal squared norm be defined by \[ \begin{align} \operatorname{off}(\bA)^2 = \sum_{i \neq j} a_{ij}^2 = \|\bA\|_F^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}^2. \end{align} \] Applying the Jacobi rotation \(\bA' = \bG(p, q, \theta)^T \bA \bG(p, q, \theta)\) with the optimal angle strictly reduces the off-diagonal norm: \[ \begin{align} \operatorname{off}(\bA')^2 = \operatorname{off}(\bA)^2 - 2a_{pq}^2. \end{align} \]

TipProof

Because \(\bG\) is orthogonal, the total Frobenius norm is preserved: \(\|\bA'\|_F^2 = \|\bA\|_F^2\). The rotation affects only entries in rows and columns \(p\) and \(q\). For the \(2 \times 2\) submatrix, the sum of squares is invariant under orthogonal similarity: \[ \begin{align} (a'_{pp})^2 + (a'_{qq})^2 + 2(a'_{pq})^2 = a_{pp}^2 + a_{qq}^2 + 2a_{pq}^2. \end{align} \] Because the rotation enforces \(a'_{pq} = 0\), the sum of squared diagonal entries increases: \[ \begin{align} (a'_{pp})^2 + (a'_{qq})^2 = a_{pp}^2 + a_{qq}^2 + 2a_{pq}^2. \end{align} \] All other diagonal entries \(a_{ii}\) (\(i \neq p, q\)) are untouched. Substituting this into \(\operatorname{off}(\bA')^2 = \|\bA'\|_F^2 - \sum (a'_{ii})^2\) proves that \(\operatorname{off}(\bA')^2 = \operatorname{off}(\bA)^2 - 2a_{pq}^2\).

NoteDefinition: Cyclic Jacobi Algorithm

Let \(\bA^{(0)}=\bA\) and \(\bV=\bI_n\). While \(\operatorname{off}(\bA)>`tol`\,\|\bA^{(0)}\|_F\), sweep through the pairs \(1\leq p<q\leq n\) in a fixed order. For each pair, compute \(c\) and \(s\) from the referenced result, replace \(\bA\) by \(\bG(p,q,\theta)^T\bA\bG(p,q,\theta)\), and replace \(\bV\) by \(\bV\bG(p,q,\theta)\). On termination, the diagonal of \(\bA\) approximates the eigenvalues and the columns of \(\bV\) the eigenvectors.

the referenced result shows that \(\operatorname{off}(\bA)^2\) decreases by \(2a_{pq}^2\) at every rotation. Choosing the largest off-diagonal entry at each step gives linear convergence, as the exercises show. The cyclic order avoids that search, and once the off-diagonal entries are small compared with the gaps between the eigenvalues, cyclic Jacobi converges quadratically, measured sweep by sweep (Golub and Van Loan 2013). Each sweep costs \(O(n^3)\) flops.

14.4 Shifted Systems from One Hessenberg Reduction

Several calculations require the solution of \((s\bI-\bA)\bx=\bb\) for many complex shifts \(s\) with the same \(\bA\) and \(\bb\). The vector \((s\bI-\bA)^{-1}\bb\) is the resolvent of \(\bA\) applied to \(\bb\). Resolvents appear in Laplace transforms of solutions of linear differential equations, in frequency responses, and in contour-integral formulas for matrix functions. Factoring \(s\bI-\bA\) separately for each of \(N\) shifts costs about \(\tfrac23n^3\) flops per shift. The Hessenberg reduction of the referenced result removes this repetition, because adding a multiple of the identity does not change Hessenberg structure.

NoteTheorem: Shifted Solves with a Hessenberg Matrix

Let \(\bA=\bQ\bH\bQ^T\) with \(\bQ\) orthogonal and \(\bH\) upper Hessenberg, and let \(s\) not be an eigenvalue of \(\bA\). Then \(\bx=(s\bI-\bA)^{-1}\bb\) is given by \[ \begin{align} (s\bI-\bH)\by=\bQ^T\bb, \qquad \bx=\bQ\by. \end{align} \] Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting solves the Hessenberg system in \(O(n^2)\) operations. At each step, the only possible row interchange is between the pivot row and the row directly below it.

TipProof

Since \(\bQ\bQ^T=\bI\), we have \(s\bI-\bA=\bQ(s\bI-\bH)\bQ^T\), so \(\bx=\bQ(s\bI-\bH)^{-1}\bQ^T\bb\). The matrix \(s\bI-\bH\) differs from \(-\bH\) only on the diagonal, so it is upper Hessenberg. Suppose that after \(k-1\) elimination steps the active submatrix is upper Hessenberg. Its first column then has a single nonzero entry below the diagonal, in row \(k+1\). Partial pivoting compares only rows \(k\) and \(k+1\), computes one multiplier, and updates one row of length at most \(n-k+1\). Rows \(k+2,...,n\) are untouched, so the next active submatrix is again upper Hessenberg. Summing the work over \(k=1,...,n-1\) gives \(O(n^2)\) operations, and the triangular solve that follows costs another \(O(n^2)\).

Reducing \(\bA\) to Hessenberg form with Householder reflectors costs about \(\tfrac{10}{3}n^3\) flops when \(\bQ\) is kept as a product of reflectors, and this reduction is done once (Golub and Van Loan 2013). Applying \(\bQ^T\) and \(\bQ\) to a vector costs \(O(n^2)\) flops. For \(N\) shifts, the total cost is therefore about \(\tfrac{10}{3}n^3+O(Nn^2)\) flops, compared with about \(\tfrac23Nn^3\) for separate factorizations. The reduction pays for itself after five shifts, and with one hundred shifts it reduces the cost by a factor of about twenty.

TipRemark

(Contour integrals for the matrix exponential) The Cauchy integral formula for matrix functions gives \[ \begin{align} e^{t\bA}\bb=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_\Gamma e^{ts}(s\bI-\bA)^{-1}\bb\,ds, \end{align} \] where \(\Gamma\) is a contour that encloses the spectrum of \(\bA\) (Higham 2008). When the spectrum lies in the left half-plane, as for Markov generators and discretized diffusion operators, \(\Gamma\) can be chosen to wrap around the negative real axis so that \(e^{ts}\) decays along it. After a parametrization of \(\Gamma\), the trapezoid rule with \(N\) nodes approximates the integral by a sum of \(N\) resolvent applications, each computed by the referenced result. For suitable Talbot-type contours the error decreases geometrically in \(N\), and a few tens of nodes commonly reach double-precision accuracy (Weideman and Trefethen 2007). The geometric convergence is the same mechanism as the spectral accuracy of the trapezoid rule for periodic analytic integrands in the referenced section. For large sparse matrices, the Krylov approximations of the referenced section are usually preferable, because they avoid the dense Hessenberg reduction.

14.5 Exercises

WarningExercise

  1. Compute. (Observed cubic convergence of RQI) Apply RQI to a symmetric matrix with distinct eigenvalues, starting from several unit vectors close to the same eigenvector.

    1. Record the angle errors \(\theta_k=\angle(\bv^{(k)},\bq_*)\) and the ratios \(\sin\theta_{k+1}/\sin^3\theta_k\).

    2. Record the Rayleigh errors and verify the cubic relation in the referenced result before rounding error dominates.

    3. Repeat with two nearly repeated eigenvalues and explain how the spectral gap enters the constants in the proof.

  2. Explore. (Failure of unshifted QR on an exchange matrix) Let \(\bP = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\).

    1. Compute the QR factorization \(\bP = \bQ_0 \bR_0\).

    2. Compute \(\bP_1 = \bR_0 \bQ_0\) and show that \(\bP_1 = \bP\).

    3. Repeat the step with a nonzero shift. Determine which shifts break the cycle, and explain how an eigenvalue shift from the trailing two by two block permits immediate deflation in this example.

  3. Compute. (Givens rotation Hessenberg QR) Write a Python function that executes one step of shifted QR on an upper Hessenberg matrix \(\bH \in \fR^{n \times n}\) using \(n-1\) Givens rotations. Verify that your implementation requires \(O(n^2)\) flops rather than \(O(n^3)\).

  4. Compute. Perform one Jacobi rotation by hand to zero out the off-diagonal entry of \[ \begin{align} \bA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \] Explicitly compute \(\tau, t, c, s\), and the resulting diagonal matrix \(\bA'\).

  5. Compute. Verify that the eigenvalues computed on the diagonal of \(\bA'\) match the exact roots of \(\det(\bA - \lambda\bI) = 0\).

  6. Derive. Prove that for an \(n \times n\) matrix, if the largest off-diagonal entry is selected at each step (Classical Jacobi), then \(\operatorname{off}(\bA^{(k+1)})^2 \leq \left( 1 - \frac{2}{n(n-1)} \right) \operatorname{off}(\bA^{(k)})^2\).

  7. Explore. (Many shifted solves) For a random \(200\times200\) matrix, compare the time to solve \((s_k\bI-\bA)\bx_k=\bb\) for \(k=1,...,N\) by separate LU factorizations and by one Hessenberg reduction followed by the referenced result. Find the value of \(N\) at which the two approaches cost the same, and compare it with the flop estimate in the text.

34  Koopman Theory, EDMD, and Continuous Generators

(Running example: Markov chains) The chemical master equation evolves probability densities, while the Koopman generator evolves observables. They are adjoint descriptions of the same Markov dynamics. This duality connects the matrix exponential, sensitivity formulas, polynomial closure, and data-driven operator recovery.

Koopman operator theory shifts the analysis of nonlinear dynamical systems from finite-dimensional nonlinear state updates to linear transformations on an infinite-dimensional space of observables. Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) and Extended DMD (EDMD) compute finite-dimensional approximations of this operator from snapshot data. In continuous-time Markov jump processes and chemical kinetics, the Koopman generator is the formal adjoint of the Chemical Master Equation, exhibiting exact polynomial closure for unimolecular systems. Recovering continuous rate matrices from discrete Koopman operators requires the matrix logarithm, which suffers from modal noise explosion on fast decaying dynamics.

34.1 Koopman Operator Theory

NoteDefinition: Nonlinear Dynamics and Observables

Consider a discrete-time dynamical system \[ \begin{align} \bx^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{F}(\bx^{(k)}), \qquad \bx \in \mathcal{M} \subseteq \fR^n. \end{align} \] An observable is a scalar function \(g: \mathcal{M} \to \fR\) belonging to a function space \(\mathcal{F}\) (such as \(L^2(\mathcal{M})\)). The Koopman operator \(\mathcal{K}: \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{F}\) is defined by \[ \begin{align} (\mathcal{K}g)(\bx) = g(\mathbf{F}(\bx)). \end{align} \] The operator \(\mathcal{K}\) is linear on \(\mathcal{F}\), even when the underlying map \(\mathbf{F}\) is nonlinear.

NoteProposition: Linearity of the Koopman Operator

The Koopman operator \(\mathcal{K}\) satisfies \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{K}(\alpha g_1 + \beta g_2) = \alpha \mathcal{K} g_1 + \beta \mathcal{K} g_2 \end{align} \] for all scalars \(\alpha, \beta \in \fR\) and observables \(g_1, g_2 \in \mathcal{F}\).

TipProof

Evaluating the composition at any state \(\bx \in \mathcal{M}\) gives \[ \begin{align} [\mathcal{K}(\alpha g_1 + \beta g_2)](\bx) &= (\alpha g_1 + \beta g_2)(\mathbf{F}(\bx)) \\ &= \alpha g_1(\mathbf{F}(\bx)) + \beta g_2(\mathbf{F}(\bx)) \\ &= \alpha [\mathcal{K}g_1](\bx) + \beta [\mathcal{K}g_2](\bx) \\ &= [\alpha \mathcal{K}g_1 + \beta \mathcal{K}g_2](\bx). \end{align} \] Because this equality holds for all \(\bx \in \mathcal{M}\), the operators are identical.

NoteDefinition: Koopman Eigenfunctions and Eigenvalues

A function \(\varphi \in \mathcal{F}\) is a Koopman eigenfunction with eigenvalue \(\mu \in \fC\) if \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{K}\varphi = \mu \varphi. \end{align} \] Along system trajectories, Koopman eigenfunctions evolve linearly: \[ \begin{align} \varphi(\bx^{(k)}) = \mu^k \varphi(\bx^{(0)}). \end{align} \] A sufficiently rich collection of Koopman eigenfunctions can provide coordinates in which part of the nonlinear dynamics evolves linearly. Such a collection need not exist globally, and finite dictionaries generally give only a projected approximation.

34.2 Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) and EDMD

NoteDefinition: Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD)

Given snapshot matrices \(\bX, \bY \in \fR^{n \times M}\) where \(\bY\) advances \(\bX\) by time step \(h\): \[ \begin{align} \bX = [\bx_1, \bx_2, ..., \bx_M], \qquad \bY = [\by_1, \by_2, ..., \by_M], \qquad \by_m = \mathbf{F}(\bx_m), \end{align} \] DMD seeks the best-fit linear map \(\bA \approx \bY \bX^\dagger\) in the Frobenius norm.

To compute the reduced model:

  1. Compute the rank-\(r\) truncated SVD \(\bX \approx \bU_r \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r \bV_r^T\).

  2. Project onto the POD subspace: \(\widetilde{\bA} = \bU_r^T \bY \bV_r \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r^{-1} \in \fR^{r \times r}\).

  3. Compute eigendecomposition \(\widetilde{\bA} \bw_i = \mu_i \bw_i\).

  4. The DMD modes are \(\boldsymbol{\phi}_i = \bY \bV_r \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r^{-1} \bw_i\).

NoteProposition: DMD as a Least-Squares Fit

The minimum-Frobenius-norm solution of \[ \begin{align} \min_{\bA}\|\bY-\bA\bX\|_F \end{align} \] is \(\bA_*=\bY\bX^\dagger\). If \(\bX=\bU_r\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r\bV_r^T\) is a rank-\(r\) SVD, the representation of this map on \(\mathcal{R}(\bU_r)\) is \[ \begin{align} \widetilde{\bA}=\bU_r^T\bA_*\bU_r =\bU_r^T\bY\bV_r\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r^{-1}. \end{align} \]

TipProof

The rows of \(\bA\bX\) are independent least-squares fits to the corresponding rows of \(\bY\). The pseudoinverse therefore gives the minimum-norm minimizer \(\bA_*=\bY\bX^\dagger\). Substituting \(\bX^\dagger=\bV_r\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r^{-1}\bU_r^T\) and projecting on both sides by \(\bU_r\) gives \[ \begin{align} \bU_r^T\bA_*\bU_r =\bU_r^T\bY\bV_r\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r^{-1}\bU_r^T\bU_r =\bU_r^T\bY\bV_r\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r^{-1}. \end{align} \]

NoteDefinition: Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)

Extended DMD (Williams et al. 2015) generalizes DMD by lifting state data through a dictionary of nonlinear observables \(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\bx) = (\psi_1(\bx), ..., \psi_K(\bx))^T \in \fR^K\).

Form data matrices \[ \begin{align} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_X = [\boldsymbol{\psi}(\bx_1), ..., \boldsymbol{\psi}(\bx_M)] \in \fR^{K \times M}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Psi}_Y = [\boldsymbol{\psi}(\by_1), ..., \boldsymbol{\psi}(\by_M)] \in \fR^{K \times M}. \end{align} \] The finite-dimensional representation \(\widehat{\bK}_h \in \fR^{K \times K}\) of the Koopman operator minimizes \(\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_Y - \bK \boldsymbol{\Psi}_X\|_F^2\): \[ \begin{align} \widehat{\bK}_h = \boldsymbol{\Psi}_Y \boldsymbol{\Psi}_X^\dagger = (\boldsymbol{\Psi}_Y \boldsymbol{\Psi}_X^T)(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_X \boldsymbol{\Psi}_X^T)^\dagger. \end{align} \] Left eigenvectors of \(\widehat{\bK}_h\) yield approximations to Koopman eigenfunctions.

TipRemark

(EDMD as a Galerkin method) When the dictionary consists of the coordinate functions, \(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\bx)=\bx\), the data matrices reduce to \(\bX\) and \(\bY\), and EDMD reduces to the least-squares map of the referenced result. For a richer dictionary, the matrices \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_Y\boldsymbol{\Psi}_X^T/M\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_X\boldsymbol{\Psi}_X^T/M\) are sample averages of inner products between dictionary functions. As the number of samples drawn from a measure \(\mu\) grows, they converge to the corresponding inner-product matrices in \(L^2(\mu)\), and \(\widehat{\bK}_h\) converges to the Galerkin projection of the Koopman operator onto the span of the dictionary (Williams et al. 2015). Enlarging the dictionary therefore raises a separate approximation question, namely how well that span represents the Koopman eigenfunctions of interest.

34.3 CME Koopman Generator and Polynomial Closure

TipRemark

(Koopman generator for continuous Markov jump processes) For a continuous-time Markov process with master equation \(\dot{\bp}(t) = \bA(\bc) \bp(t)\), the expectation of an observable \(g(\bx)\) evolves according to \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[g(\bx(t))] = \frac{d}{dt} \sum_{\bx} g(\bx) p(\bx, t) = \sum_{\bx} g(\bx) [\bA(\bc)\bp(t)]_{\bx} = \sum_{\bx} [\bA(\bc)^T g](\bx) p(\bx, t)\). The Koopman infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A}\) is the formal adjoint of the master operator: \(\mathcal{A} = \bA(\bc)^T\).

NoteDefinition: CME Koopman Generator

For a chemical reaction network with stoichiometry \(\bnu_k\) and propensities \(\alpha_k(\bx; \bc) = c_k h_k(\bx)\), the Koopman generator acts on observable \(g\) by \[ \begin{align} (\mathcal{A}g)(\bx) = \sum_{k=1}^R \alpha_k(\bx; \bc) \Big( g(\bx + \bnu_k) - g(\bx) \Big). \end{align} \]

NoteTheorem: Polynomial Closure in Chemical Kinetics

Under mass-action kinetics, propensity functions are falling factorial polynomials: \[ \begin{align} h_k(\bx) = \prod_{s=1}^S x_s^{(\nu_{ks}^-)}, \qquad x_s^{(m)} = x_s(x_s - 1)\cdots(x_s - m + 1). \end{align} \] Let \(\mathcal{P}_d\) denote the space of polynomials in \(\bx\) of degree at most \(d\).

  1. Unimolecular networks: If all reactions have reactant order at most 1 (reactions of form \(\emptyset \to X_i\) or \(X_i \to X_j\)), then \(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_d \subseteq \mathcal{P}_d\) for all \(d \geq 0\). The subspace \(\mathcal{P}_d\) is invariant under \(\mathcal{A}\), yielding exact finite-dimensional closure.

  2. Bimolecular networks: For reactions of reactant order at most 2, \(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_d \subseteq \mathcal{P}_{d+1}\). A quadratic propensity can produce degree-\((d+1)\) terms, so the moment equations generally fail to close at degree \(d\) unless additional cancellations, conservation laws, or invariant subspaces are present.

TipProof

Let \(g \in \mathcal{P}_d\) be a polynomial of degree \(d\). For each stoichiometric change vector \(\bnu_k\), the discrete difference operator expands via Taylor’s formula on polynomials: \[ \begin{align} g(\bx + \bnu_k) - g(\bx) = \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{1}{j!} (\bnu_k \cdot \nabla)^j g(\bx). \end{align} \] Because differentiation reduces polynomial degree by at least one, each term in the sum has degree at most \(d - 1\), so \(g(\bx + \bnu_k) - g(\bx) \in \mathcal{P}_{d-1}\). Applying the Koopman generator (the referenced result): \[ \begin{align} (\mathcal{A}g)(\bx) = \sum_{k=1}^R \alpha_k(\bx; \bc) \big( g(\bx + \bnu_k) - g(\bx) \big). \end{align} \] For unimolecular reactions, each propensity \(\alpha_k(\bx; \bc)\) has reactant order at most 1, so \(\alpha_k \in \mathcal{P}_1\). The product of a degree-1 polynomial and a degree-\((d-1)\) polynomial has degree at most \(1 + (d - 1) = d\), proving \(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_d \subseteq \mathcal{P}_d\). For bimolecular reactions, reactant order is at most 2, so \(\alpha_k \in \mathcal{P}_2\). The product has degree at most \(2 + (d - 1) = d + 1\), giving \(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_d \subseteq \mathcal{P}_{d+1}\).

34.4 Continuous Rate Recovery and Logarithmic Noise Explosion

TipRemark

(Discrete-to-continuous inversion) Given an EDMD estimate of the discrete Koopman operator \(\widehat{\bK}_h \approx \exp(h\mathcal{A})\) over a sampling interval \(h\), the continuous generator is recovered via the principal matrix logarithm: \[ \begin{align} \widehat{\bA} = \frac{1}{h} \operatorname{logm}(\widehat{\bK}_h). \end{align} \] If \(\widehat{\bK}_h\) is real and has no eigenvalues on the closed negative real axis \((-\infty,0]\), then its principal matrix logarithm exists and is real. A matrix with eigenvalues on this branch cut may possess other logarithms, but it does not possess the principal logarithm used here.

NoteTheorem: Logarithmic Noise Explosion on Fast Decay Modes

Let \(\lambda_i < 0\) be a continuous eigenvalue of the generator \(\mathcal{A}\), corresponding to a discrete Koopman eigenvalue \(\mu_i = \exp(h \lambda_i) \in (0, 1)\). The relation between discrete and continuous eigenvalues is \[ \begin{align} \lambda_i = \frac{1}{h} \ln \mu_i. \end{align} \] The sensitivity of the continuous eigenvalue with respect to perturbations in the discrete eigenvalue is \[ \begin{align} \left|\frac{d\lambda_i}{d\mu_i}\right| = \frac{1}{h \mu_i} = \frac{1}{h \exp(h \lambda_i)}. \end{align} \] For fast decaying modes where \(-\lambda_i \gg 1\), \(\mu_i \to 0\) exponentially fast. Consequently, the noise amplification factor diverges: \[ \begin{align} \lim_{-\lambda_i \to \infty} \left|\frac{d\lambda_i}{d\mu_i}\right| = \infty. \end{align} \] A tiny statistical fluctuation \(\Delta \mu_i\) in data-driven EDMD induces an exponentially magnified error \(\Delta \lambda_i\) in the recovered rate.

TipProof

Differentiating \(\lambda_i = \frac{1}{h}\ln \mu_i\) directly with respect to \(\mu_i\) yields \(\frac{d\lambda_i}{d\mu_i} = \frac{1}{h\mu_i}\). Substituting \(\mu_i = \exp(h\lambda_i)\) produces \(\frac{1}{h} \exp(-h\lambda_i)\). Because \(h > 0\) and \(\lambda_i < 0\), \(-\lambda_i > 0\), so \(\exp(-h\lambda_i) \to \infty\) as \(-\lambda_i \to \infty\).

34.5 Exercises

WarningExercise

  1. Check. (Koopman linearity) Consider the one-dimensional system \(x_{k+1} = x_k^3\). Let \(g_1(x) = x\) and \(g_2(x) = x^3\).

    1. Compute \((\mathcal{K}g_1)(x)\) and \((\mathcal{K}g_2)(x)\).

    2. Verify that while \(x \mapsto x^3\) is nonlinear, \(\mathcal{K}(\alpha g_1 + \beta g_2) = \alpha \mathcal{K}g_1 + \beta \mathcal{K}g_2\).

  2. Compute. (DMD residual) Compute \(\bA_*=\bY\bX^\dagger\) for a rank-one snapshot matrix and verify directly that the residual \(\bY-\bA_*\bX\) is orthogonal to the row space of \(\bX\), as predicted by the referenced result.

  3. Derive. (Polynomial closure in unimolecular reaction networks) Consider the reversible isomerization network \(A \underset{c_2}{\overset{c_1}{\rightleftharpoons}} B\). Let \(x_1, x_2\) denote the copy numbers of species \(A\) and \(B\).

    1. Write down the propensities \(\alpha_1(\bx) = c_1 x_1\) and \(\alpha_2(\bx) = c_2 x_2\), and the stoichiometry vectors \(\bnu_1 = (-1, 1)^T\) and \(\bnu_2 = (1, -1)^T\).

    2. Apply the Koopman generator \(\mathcal{A}\) to the linear observable \(g(\bx) = x_1\). Show that \((\mathcal{A}g)(\bx) = -c_1 x_1 + c_2 x_2\), which is strictly linear.

    3. Apply \(\mathcal{A}\) to the second-order observable \(g(\bx) = x_1^2\). Show that \((\mathcal{A}g)(\bx)\) contains only polynomials of degree at most 2.

    4. Explain why the subspace of polynomials of degree at most \(d\) is invariant under \(\mathcal{A}\) for any unimolecular network.

  4. Derive. (Bimolecular moment hierarchy) Consider the dimerization reaction \(2A \xrightarrow{c} B\). The stoichiometry is \(\bnu = (-2, 1)^T\) and the propensity is \(\alpha(x) = c x(x-1)/2\).

    1. Compute \((\mathcal{A}g)(x)\) for \(g(x) = x\). Show that the result is quadratic in \(x\).

    2. Explain why computing the time derivative of the mean \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[x]\) requires knowledge of the second moment \(\mathbb{E}[x^2]\).

  5. Explore. (Logarithmic noise explosion on fast decay modes) Suppose a continuous dynamical system has decay rates \(\lambda_1 = -1\) and \(\lambda_2 = -50\). Data snapshots are collected at sampling interval \(h = 0.1\).

    1. Compute the true discrete eigenvalues \(\mu_1 = \exp(h\lambda_1)\) and \(\mu_2 = \exp(h\lambda_2)\).

    2. Suppose statistical noise perturbs the discrete estimates by \(\Delta \mu = 10^{-3}\). Compute the resulting perturbation \(\Delta \lambda_1 \approx \frac{\Delta \mu}{h \mu_1}\).

    3. Compute the resulting perturbation \(\Delta \lambda_2 \approx \frac{\Delta \mu}{h \mu_2}\).

    4. Explain why recovering continuous generators from discrete snapshots requires regularized regression rather than unconstrained matrix logarithms.
